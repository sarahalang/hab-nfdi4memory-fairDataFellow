# Corpus Visualizations: Alchemical Laboratory Objects Dataset

Documentation for the 25 figures generated by `visualize_corpus.py`, analyzing the metadata of the 98 source books in the alchemical laboratory objects dataset.

**Data source:** `master_metadata.csv` (98 rows, 51 columns). See `README.md` for the full column reference and provenance pipeline.

**Technical note on author names:** Several figures use a derived field `author_display`, which picks the best available author name for each entry using this priority: `author_likely_identical_shared` (canonical group name from ChatGPT) > `author_normalized_inferred` (ChatGPT-improved form) > `author_normalized` (mechanically normalized). This ensures that variant spellings for the same person (e.g., "Athanasius Kircherus" and "Athanasius Kircher") are counted together.

**Rendering:** All figures are saved at 150 DPI as PNG files in the `figures/` directory. The script uses `bbox_inches="tight"` on all saves to prevent label clipping.

## 1. Books per decade

![Books per decade](figures/01_books_per_decade.png)

### Technical details

- **Chart type:** Stacked bar chart.
- **X axis:** Decade (derived from `year_normalized`, computed as `year // 10 * 10`). Labels show "1500s", "1510s", etc.
- **Y axis:** Number of books.
- **Stacking:** Each bar is split into two segments by `source_collection`: CHR2023 (blue) and HAB (red). CHR2023 is drawn first (bottom), HAB stacked on top.
- **Missing data:** 1 of 98 entries lacks a year and is excluded.
- **Assumptions:** The decade is computed from `year_normalized`, which is the best available publication year (manual > scraped > original). For approximate dates, the single best-guess year is used.

### What it shows

This figure shows when the books in the dataset were published, decade by decade, and which of the two source collections (CHR2023 or HAB) they come from. The dataset covers roughly 250 years of publishing, from the 1500s to the 1750s. The peak is in the 1610s, and there is a noticeable dip in the 1660s–1670s. The CHR2023 corpus contributes books across most decades but is smaller overall; the HAB collection provides the bulk of the material, especially in the 17th century.

## 2. Top 15 publication places

![Top 15 publication places](figures/02_publication_places.png)

### Technical details

- **Chart type:** Horizontal bar chart.
- **Data field:** `place_normalized` — publication places mapped to modern English city names via a ~90-entry lookup table (e.g., "Francofurti" → "Frankfurt am Main", "Argentorati" → "Strasbourg").
- **Filtering:** Entries with no place information are excluded.
- **Metric:** Simple count of books per place. Bars are sorted by frequency, descending.
- **Labels:** Exact count shown to the right of each bar.

### What it shows

Frankfurt am Main dominates the corpus with 27 books — more than the next two cities (Strasbourg: 10, Nuremberg: 8) combined. This reflects Frankfurt's historical role as the center of the German book trade and the location of major book fairs. The list includes major early modern printing centers across the German-speaking world (Frankfurt, Strasbourg, Nuremberg, Basel, Leipzig, Cologne), the Low Countries (Amsterdam, Leiden), Italy (Venice, Bologna), Switzerland (Zurich, Geneva), and France (Paris, Lyon). The single London entry represents an English translation of Brunschwig's distillation manual.

## 3. Language distribution

![Language distribution](figures/03_languages.png)

### Technical details

- **Chart type:** Pie chart.
- **Data field:** `language_normalized` — full language name. For HAB entries (which lacked language metadata), this was inferred by ChatGPT from the title and content of each book. For CHR2023 entries, it was mapped from original language codes (DE, EN, LAT, IT).
- **Filtering:** Entries with empty language are excluded (none in current data — all 98 have a language).
- **Categories:** German, Latin, German + Latin (bilingual works), Italian, French, English, uncertain.
- **Assumptions:** The "uncertain" category includes entries where the language could not be confidently determined (e.g., a Latin title page but German body text). "German + Latin" denotes genuinely bilingual publications, not books that merely have a Latin title.

### What it shows

German and Latin together account for the vast majority of the corpus. German is the most common single language, reflecting the heavy representation of practical handbooks (distillation manuals, assay books). Latin dominates among scholarly and encyclopedic works. The "German + Latin" category captures bilingual editions common in the period, where Latin was used for learned apparatus alongside German practical text. Italian works are almost all editions of Biringuccio's *Pirotechnia*. The single English entry is Brunschwig's *Liber de arte distillandi*, translated and published in London in 1527.

## 4. Authors with 2+ books

![Authors with 2+ books](figures/04_top_authors.png)

### Technical details

- **Chart type:** Horizontal bar chart.
- **Data field:** `author_display` (see note at top). This groups variant spellings: e.g., both "Athanasius Kircherus" and "Athanasius Kircher" count toward the same author.
- **Filtering:** Only authors with 2 or more books in the dataset. Authors listed as empty string are excluded. "anonymous" is included as a category.
- **Metric:** Count of books per author.
- **Assumptions:** The `author_likely_identical_shared` field was assigned by ChatGPT and manually reviewed. Pseudepigraphic attributions are preserved (e.g., "Pseudo-Geber" counts as a single author, distinct from the historical Jabir ibn Hayyan).

### What it shows

Johann Kunckel and Vannoccio Biringuccio lead with 5 books each — in both cases because the dataset includes multiple editions of their major works. Athanasius Kircher, Hieronymus Brunschwig, and Giambattista della Porta follow with 4 each. The chart reveals the "long tail": 22 authors appear at least twice, but most have exactly 2 entries (often two editions or a translation). The presence of "anonymous" (2 entries) reflects the tradition of anonymous technical handbooks, particularly the *Probierbüchlein* (assay manual) tradition.

## 5. Pages per book (HAB)

![Pages per book](figures/05_pages_per_book.png)

### Technical details

- **Chart type:** Two-panel figure. Left: histogram with logarithmic x-axis. Right: horizontal bar chart (top 10).
- **Data field:** `pages_included_count` — the number of digitized pages from each book included in the computer vision dataset. This is only available for HAB entries (73 books); CHR2023 entries lack this field.
- **Filtering:** HAB entries only, with valid page count.
- **Left panel bins:** [1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 50, 110] — log-scale bins to show the skewed distribution.
- **Right panel:** Top 10 HAB books by number of pages included, with truncated short titles.

### What it shows

Most HAB books contribute only a few pages to the CV dataset (median around 2–3 pages), but a few books contribute disproportionately. Libavius's *Alchymia* (1606) is the largest single source with 104 pages — a massive illustrated encyclopedia of alchemy. The right panel shows that a handful of extensively illustrated books provide the bulk of the image data. This is important context for anyone using the CV dataset: the training images are not evenly distributed across source books.

## 6. Edition groups over time

![Edition groups over time](figures/06_edition_groups.png)

### Technical details

- **Chart type:** Scatter plot with connecting horizontal lines.
- **Data fields:** `short_title_likely_identical_shared` (canonical work title for grouping editions), `year_normalized`.
- **Filtering:** Only works with 2+ entries in the dataset. 17 such groups were identified, covering 45 of 98 entries.
- **Y axis:** Each row represents one work group, sorted by earliest publication year.
- **X axis:** Publication year. Dots mark individual editions; lines connect the earliest and latest edition.
- **Colors:** Each work has a distinct color (tab20 colormap).
- **Assumptions:** The "likely identical" groupings were established by ChatGPT and manually reviewed. They link different editions, translations, and reprints of the same underlying work.

### What it shows

This figure shows which works appear multiple times in the dataset as different editions or translations, and how they spread over time. Some works had remarkably long publishing lives: Biringuccio's *Pirotechnia* spans 128 years (1550–1678), and Agricola's *De re metallica* spans 100 years (1557–1657). Others cluster tightly (Mundus subterraneus: 4 entries all from 1665, representing different volumes or issues of the same Amsterdam edition). The connecting lines make it easy to see which works persisted across generations.

## 8. Collection overview

![Collection overview](figures/08_collection_overview.png)

### Technical details

- **Chart type:** Three-panel bar chart.
- **Panel 1 — Books per collection:** Count of entries by `source_collection` (CHR2023 vs HAB).
- **Panel 2 — Metadata completeness:** Count by `metadata_completeness` field, which classifies each entry as "complete" (all 4 key fields: author, year, publisher, place), "partial" (2–3 of 4), or "minimal" (0–1). The value `[S.l.]` (sine loco, "without place") is treated as missing.
- **Panel 3 — Books per century:** Count by `century` field (derived: years 1500–1599 = "16th c.", etc.).
- **Exact counts** are labeled above each bar.

### What it shows

A high-level summary of the dataset composition. The HAB collection (73 books) is roughly three times the size of the CHR2023 corpus (25 books). Metadata quality is high: 86 of 98 entries have complete bibliographic information; the 11 "partial" entries are mostly anonymous works where no author could be identified; 1 entry is "minimal" (an undated anonymous *Probierbüchlein*). The 17th century is the best-represented period (53 books), followed by the 16th century (33) and the 18th century (11 — only its first half, since the dataset ends around 1750).

## 9. Place × decade heatmap

![Place × decade heatmap](figures/09_place_decade_heatmap.png)

### Technical details

- **Chart type:** Heatmap (imshow).
- **Data fields:** `place_normalized` (rows) × decade (columns, from `year_normalized`).
- **Filtering:** Only the top 10 publication places by total count. Entries missing place or year are excluded.
- **Color scale:** YlOrRd (yellow to red). Cell values annotated where > 0. Text color switches to white for high values (> 60% of maximum) for readability.
- **Row order:** By total book count (most prolific city at top).

### What it shows

This heatmap reveals the temporal profile of each major publishing center. Frankfurt am Main shows sustained activity across the entire period but peaks in the 1600s–1640s. Strasbourg is concentrated in the early 16th century (the era of Brunschwig and Grüninger) with a later resurgence. Venice's activity is limited to the mid-16th century (Biringuccio's editions). Amsterdam appears only in the 1650s–1660s (Kircher's works, Janssonius publishing house). Nuremberg emerges late (1680s–1700s) as a center for German-language practical manuals. This pattern reflects real shifts in the geography of alchemical publishing.

## 13. Edition geographic spread

![Edition geographic spread](figures/13_edition_spread.png)

### Technical details

- **Chart type:** Scatter plot with connecting lines and inline labels.
- **X axis:** `year_normalized` (publication year).
- **Y axis:** `place_normalized` (publication city), ordered by frequency.
- **Data:** Only works with 2+ entries in the dataset (`short_title_likely_identical_shared` groupings).
- **Lines:** Connect editions of the same work chronologically, showing geographic migration.
- **Labels:** Each work's title is placed near its latest edition (to avoid crowding in the dense early cluster). A collision-avoidance algorithm tries multiple offset positions.
- **Colors:** Each work has a distinct color (tab20 colormap).

### What it shows

This is one of the most information-rich figures. Each line traces how a single work traveled across cities over time as it was reprinted, re-edited, or translated. Biringuccio's *Pirotechnia* starts in Venice (1550), moves to Paris as a French translation (1556), returns to Venice for Italian reprintings (1559), and finally appears in Bologna (1678) — a journey spanning 128 years and 4 cities. Brunschwig's distillation manual travels from Strasbourg to London (an English translation). Agricola's *De re metallica* stays in Basel but spans a century. Works that don't move geographically (like Kircher's *Mundus subterraneus*, all printed in Amsterdam) appear as horizontal clusters.

## 14. Publisher × place heatmap

![Publisher × place heatmap](figures/14_publisher_place.png)

### Technical details

- **Chart type:** Heatmap (imshow).
- **Data fields:** `publisher_normalized_inferred` (rows) × `place_normalized` (columns).
- **Filtering:** Only publishers with 2+ books. All-zero columns dropped.
- **Color scale:** PuBuGn (purple-blue-green).

### What it shows

This confirms what we would expect from early modern book history: publishers were overwhelmingly local. Each publisher operates in exactly one city. Zieger is in Nuremberg, Grüninger in Strasbourg, Janssonius & Weyerstraten in Amsterdam, Lucas Jennis in Frankfurt, and so on. There are no multi-city publishers in this dataset — unlike modern publishing houses, early modern printers were tied to a single workshop and local market. This means that when a work appears in a new city, it almost always involved a new publisher.

## 18. Edition detail: place, publisher, language

![Edition detail](figures/18_edition_detail.png)

### Technical details

- **Chart type:** Two-panel layout. Left panel: timeline scatter (dots colored by language). Right panel: tabular details (year, place, publisher, language) in monospace text.
- **Data:** All works with 2+ entries in the `short_title_likely_identical_shared` groupings. Within each group, editions are sorted by year.

The right-hand table makes it possible to read exact details that would be illegible as scatter plot labels.

## 19. Translation flows

![Translation flows](figures/19_translation_flows.png)

### Technical details

- **Chart type:** Network/flow diagram with arrows.
- **Data:** For each multi-edition work group, the language of the earliest edition is treated as the "original language" and any subsequent editions in a different language as "translations."
- **Nodes:** Languages appear on both left ("Original language") and right ("Translation language") sides.
- **Arrows:** Connect original → translation language. Arrow color matches the original language.
- **Labels:** Work titles appear in white boxes along each arrow path.
- **Assumptions:** The earliest edition in the dataset is assumed to represent the original language. This is generally correct but could be misleading if the dataset happens to include a translation but not the original (unlikely but not verified).

### What it shows

This diagram summarizes the directionality of translations in the corpus. German works were translated into Latin (De re metallica, Hortus sanitatis), into English (Liber de arte distillandi), and from German + Latin into pure Latin or German. Italian works moved to French (Pirotechnia). French works were translated into German (Magia naturalis). Latin works were also translated into other languages. The arrow pattern shows that German ↔ Latin was the most common translation axis, consistent with the bilingual scholarly culture of the Holy Roman Empire. Italian → French reflects Mediterranean knowledge exchange, while German → English shows the early transmission of practical knowledge to England.

## 21. Edition longevity

![Edition longevity](figures/21_edition_longevity.png)

### Technical details

- **Chart type:** Horizontal bar chart (Gantt-style).
- **Data:** For each work with 2+ editions, the bar spans from the first to the last publication year in the dataset.
- **Metric:** Time span in years (last edition year − first edition year). Number of editions also shown.
- **White dots:** Mark the year of each individual edition within the span.
- **Color scale:** Viridis, mapped to span length (longer = brighter/yellow).
- **Sorting:** By span length, descending.
- **Caveat:** This shows the span covered *within this dataset*, not the full publication history of each work. A work may have had editions before or after the ones included here.

### What it shows

Biringuccio's *Pirotechnia* has the longest documented span at 128 years (1550–1678, 5 editions), followed by Agricola's *De re metallica* at 100 years (3 editions) and della Porta's *Magia naturalis* at 89 years (2 editions). These were foundational technical works that remained relevant across generations. At the other end, *Mundus subterraneus* and *Bibliotheca chemica curiosa* show 0-year spans — multiple entries from the same year, representing different volumes or simultaneous printings rather than true re-editions. The white dots within each bar reveal whether editions were evenly spaced or clustered.

## 23. Author geographic reach

![Author geographic reach](figures/23_author_geographic_reach.png)

### Technical details

- **Chart type:** Horizontal bar chart.
- **Metric:** Number of distinct publication cities per author (from `place_normalized`).
- **Filtering:** Authors with 2+ books.
- **Sorting:** By number of cities (descending), then by number of books.
- **Labels:** City names listed to the right of each bar.
- **Colors:** Red (3+ cities), yellow (2 cities), green (1 city).
- **Caveat:** Multi-city reach often reflects translations or posthumous editions rather than the author personally arranging publication in multiple cities.

### What it shows

Giambattista della Porta has the widest geographic reach (4 cities), followed by Biringuccio and Brunschwig (3 cities each). These are authors whose works were translated and reprinted far beyond their original place of publication. By contrast, authors like Kircher (only Amsterdam), Manget (only Geneva), and Agricola (only Basel) had their works published in a single city — either because they worked closely with one publisher or because their works were not translated in the period covered. The distinction between "single-city" and "multi-city" authors roughly maps onto the distinction between regional and international circulation.

## 24. City activity timelines

![City activity timelines](figures/24_city_activity_timeline.png)

### Technical details

- **Chart type:** Multi-panel figure with one subplot per city (similar layout to Figure 20).
- **Data fields:** `place_normalized`, `year_normalized`, `short_title`, `author_display`, `language_normalized`.

Frankfurt am Main — the largest section with 27 books — reveals a diverse mix: Egenolff's practical handbooks in the 1540s–60s, alchemical compendia by Jennis and Schönwetter in the 1610s–20s, and Croll's and Maier's Latin works. ## 25. Language proportions over time

![Language proportions over time](figures/25_language_proportions.png)

### Technical details

- **Chart type:** Two-panel figure. Top: normalized (100%) stacked bar chart. Bottom: total book count per period.
- **Time binning:** 25-year periods (quarter-centuries: 1500–1524, 1525–1549, etc.), chosen to provide smoother proportions than decades while maintaining temporal resolution.
- **Language simplification:** Same categories as Figure 12.
- **Top panel:** Each bar sums to 100%, showing the proportion of each language within that period.
- **Bottom panel:** Shows the absolute number of books per period — essential context for interpreting the proportions. A period with 3 books can produce a misleading 100% bar.
- **Colors:** Standard language palette.

### What it shows

This is the proportional companion to Figure 12. The top panel reveals the shifting linguistic landscape of alchemical publishing. In the earliest period (1500–1524), German and Latin share the field equally. In 1525–1549, German dominates (practical handbooks by Brunschwig, Ryff). Italian appears in 1550–1574 (Biringuccio) and then disappears. Latin's share grows steadily through the 17th century, peaking around 1625–1649 — the era of Croll, Maier, and the rise of academic chymistry. German makes a strong comeback in 1675–1699 (Kunckel's practical manuals). The bottom panel is crucial: the 1750–1774 bar shows 100% German + Latin, but this represents only 1 book — a reminder that proportions from small samples can be misleading.
